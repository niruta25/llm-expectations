The situation

A number that sounds fine, and no way to check it

Teams now use language models to label, summarise and categorise huge piles of text. The model always answers. It always sounds confident. Nobody can read the pile to find out whether it was right.

Why the usual answers fail

Three habits that feel like checking

What people doWhy it doesn't work
Read 50 at random Mistakes are uncommon and they clump together. A random handful usually turns up nothing, which reads as good news.
Quote an accuracy score If 70% of your items belong to one category, a model that always guesses that category scores 70% and knows nothing.
Add a second AI to grade the first Nobody graded the second one. A grader that approves everything looks perfectly healthy from the outside.

What it does

Three jobs, in order of how much they save you

Ranks

Which ones to open first

Instead of "79% accurate", you get an ordered list and a straight answer: read the top 500 and you will find just over half the mistakes.

Screens

Checks the grader

A grader that approves 94% of everything passes every other test ever written. One small table catches it, before you trust a single number.

Diagnoses

Finds broken categories

Sometimes two of your categories mean nearly the same thing, and nobody — human or model — can tell them apart. No prompt fixes that. This says so.

The idea underneath

Three answers, not two

Almost every quality tool reports pass or fail. That forces a lie: when something was never actually checked — the budget ran out, the grader timed out, the item wasn't sampled — it quietly gets filed under pass.

✓

Pass

We checked it. It's fine.

✗

Fail

We checked it. It's wrong, and here's the evidence.

○

Not checked

We did not look at this one, and here is exactly why. It never turns into a pass.

That third mark is the whole product. A report that quietly stops checking and still comes back green is worse than no report at all.

Three things happen there that you won't get elsewhere. It refuses to report numbers it can't stand behind. It tells you one of your graders is asleep. And it points at a pair of categories that are the actual problem — not the model.

The difference

Before and after

What you knewWhat you know now
"It's 79% accurate." Review these 500 and you'll catch a fifth of the errors. Review 2,500 and you'll catch half.
"Our AI grader says quality is good." One of your three graders approves 94% of everything. Its opinion has been excluded.
"The model keeps getting these two wrong." Those two categories overlap. The model isn't the problem; the definitions are.
"Everything's green." Four numbers couldn't be computed, and here's what would unlock each one.

Fit

Who this is for

You sort text at scale

Support conversations into reasons. Documents into types. Feedback into themes. Hundreds or thousands at a time.

You have an AI grading an AI

Already running a model to check another model's work, and no way to know whether the checker is any good.

You have no labelled data

Most of the tool works with zero human answers. It's clear about which parts don't, and how few you'd need.

It does not label anything for you. It checks work that already exists — whatever produced it.

Honest status

The design is finished and published. The code is not written yet. A tool whose entire argument is "say what you can't stand behind" would be a poor one to oversell.
